abstract: "Background: With recent advances in technology, multiomic data have proliferated rapidly. Biobanks are now developing genomic, proteomic, lipidomic, and metabolomic data sets on the order of hundreds of thousands of people. However, bridging the gap between population-level data and the individual--a major challenge in precision medicine--remains a novel and challenging task.\n\nMethods: We analyzed the landscape of contemporary multiomics data and the drivers of population and personal molecular variability using a longitudinal cohort uniquely suited to precision health applications. Using healthy routine visit data (94 participants, median five visits per participant), we profiled the human multiome across eight molecular omic types and 1.2 million measurements, with replication in a sister multiomic cohort (322 participants).\n\nResults: Molecular data tended to cluster by omes, with the metabolome and proteome showing less intraomic correlation than other data types. While most blood-based molecular measurements followed log-normal distributions, between 2.0% and 8.1% of molecules showed evidence of bimodality, with cases driven by genetic polymorphisms or dietary differences (e.g., SERPINA5 [P = 1.4e-6], 2-piperidinone [P = 5.7e-5]). Molecular variation across the population was generally higher than that within an individual over time (e.g., xenobiotic metabolites, progestin steroids). Analyzing personal molecular variation identified several participants with notable temporal shifts (intraindividual CV > 4 SDs higher than population CV) in free fatty acids (FFA 20:1 and FFA 20:2) or cytokines (IL-17F and IFN-beta), which were unlikely to be detected cross-sectionally. Body mass index (BMI) had a much larger multiomic footprint (23.6% of molecules associated) than demographic factors like sex or age (5.7% and 1.9%, respectively), although machine-learning predictive models of sex (top ROC AUC of 0.98) were more successful than those for BMI (top R2 of 0.59 by cytokines) or age (top R2 of 0.34 by metabolites). Purely multiomic predictive models explained modest to high proportions of variation (R2 = 0.22 to 0.94) in 20 common clinical tests, highlighting the potential clinical utility of these data even within a modestly sized cohort.\n\nConclusion: Analysis of the molecular distributions, variation, and covariation of longitudinal multiomics data revealed clinically relevant differences at both the population and personal levels. Based on these findings, we conclude with six key considerations for the next generation of population omics as the field advances toward the goals of precision medicine."
