abstract: "The gut microbiome composition varies across human populations, but its characteristics and determinants in a multi-ethnic setting remain incompletely understood. Singapore, a multicultural city-state, provides a context in which culturally diverse groups share a broadly similar built environment. Using the Health for Life in Singapore (HELIOS) cohort, we profiled the gut microbiome of ethnic Chinese, Indian, and Malay participants (n = 861) residing in the country. These ethnicity-diet-microbiome relationships were further evaluated using data from two independent Malaysian cohorts (n = 544), a cohort from the United States (n = 210), and an in vitro microbial culture model. Each group displayed ethnicity-associated microbial patterns that paralleled culturally rooted dietary habits. Specifically, ethnic Indian participants showed enrichment of multiple Bifidobacterium species associated with greater intake of traditional grain-based staples such as idli and thosai; ethnic Malay participants exhibited higher abundance of Ruminococcaceae associated with coconut- and rice-based dishes; and ethnic Chinese participants had greater levels of Bacteroides associated with seafood- and meat-rich diets. These ethnicity-diet-microbiome relationships showed directionally consistent genus-level Bifidobacterium patterns in the Malaysian and US cohorts, as well as experimental support from an in vitro microbial culture model. Analysis of fecal microbiome-based risk scores revealed signature-dependent ethnic patterns in colorectal neoplasia scores. Together, these findings characterize gut microbiome variations across major Asian ethnicities residing in a shared urban environment, and provide a reference for precision health strategies in multi-ethnic Asian populations and other urban settings where culturally diverse communities share similar built environments."
